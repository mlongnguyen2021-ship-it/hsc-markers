Definition
Asexual reproduction is the production of new individuals without gametes (sex cells). It involves
one parent, and offspring are genetically identical (clones) to the parent. This happens through
mitosis.
Types of Asexual Reproduction (Vegetative Propagation)
New plants can grow from parts of the parent plant, such as stems, roots, or leaves.
Type Description Example
Stolons Stems grow along the surface and form roots + new Strawberries,
(Runners) shoots at nodes. spinifex grass
Tubers Swollen underground stems that store food; new plants Potatoes
grow from buds ("eyes").

Rhizomes Underground stems that send out shoots at nodes. Ginger, grasses
Suckers New shoots grow from the root system of the parent Colony wattle
plant.
Advantages of Asexual Reproduction
1. Fast and efficient – no need for pollination, fertilisation, or seed dispersal.
2. Only one parent needed – no energy spent finding a mate.
3. Rapid population growth – useful for recolonisation after fire/drought.
4. Offspring identical to well-adapted parent – ensures survival in stable environments.
Australian examples:
• Spinifex grass – spreads by runners, thriving in harsh, sandy, arid environments.
• Colony wattle – regrows quickly after bushfires using root suckers.
Disadvantages of Asexual Reproduction
1. No genetic variation – offspring are clones.
2. Low adaptability – if the environment changes (e.g., new disease or temperature change),
the entire population can die.
When It’s Advantageous
Asexual reproduction is beneficial in stable environments or harsh conditions where:
• There’s limited water, few pollinators, or time constraints for sexual reproduction.
• Plants need to repopulate quickly after disturbance (fire, drought).
Example Answer:
In arid areas like sand dunes, spinifex grass reproduces asexually through runners (stolons).
This is advantageous because the conditions are harsh and unpredictable, so rapid and energy-
efficient reproduction helps the plant survive and spread without needing pollinators.
How Asexual Reproduction Ensures Continuity
Asexual reproduction ensures continuity of a species by producing many identical offspring
quickly, allowing the species to survive and maintain population numbers even when
conditions are harsh or mates are unavailable.

Key Notes Summary
ASEXUAL REPRODUCTION IN FUNGI
Budding (e.g. Yeast)
• Process: A small bud forms on the parent cell → DNA replicates → nucleus divides → one
nucleus moves into the bud → bud grows → detaches to form new identical yeast cell.
• Example: Saccharomyces cerevisiae (baker’s yeast)
• Advantage: Rapid reproduction in favourable conditions.
• Disadvantage: No genetic variation — entire population vulnerable to environmental
change.
Spores (e.g. Moulds, Mushrooms)
• Process: Sporangia at the tips of hyphae produce haploid spores → spores are dispersed (by
wind) → germinate in suitable conditions → form new mycelium (fungal body).
• Advantages:
o Very large numbers of spores produced.
o Lightweight — can travel far.
o Quick colonisation of new areas.

• Disadvantage: All offspring are genetically identical — vulnerable to new diseases or climate
change.
ASEXUAL REPRODUCTION IN BACTERIA
Binary Fission
• Process:
1. Cell grows to full size.
2. DNA replicates.
3. Each DNA strand attaches to opposite ends of the cell.
4. Cell membrane pinches in, forming two identical daughter cells.
5. Each cell grows to adult size and divides again.
• Example: Escherichia coli (E. coli)
• Advantage: Extremely fast population growth (can double every 20 minutes).
• Disadvantage: No variation; one disease can wipe out the population.
ASEXUAL REPRODUCTION IN PROTISTS
Binary Fission (e.g. Amoeba)
• Process:
1. Nucleus divides by mitosis (spindle forms).
2. Cytoplasm divides (cytokinesis).
3. Two identical daughter cells form.
• Special Case: In unfavourable conditions, amoeba forms a cyst and performs multiple
fission, releasing many offspring later.
• Advantage: Efficient and allows survival in changing environments through cysts.
Budding (e.g. Hydra, some protists)
• Process: A bud forms on the parent cell → grows and detaches as a clone.
• Advantage: Rapid multiplication in suitable environments.

Sexual Reproduction
Sexual reproduction involves the fusion of genetic material from two
parents. As a result, it produces offspring that are genetically
unique. That is, offspring that are different from each other and from
their parents.
Advantages

All of the advantages of sexual reproduction come down to the fact that
it increases genetic diversity. Essentially, genetic diversity refers to
when there are lots of different versions of traits, and combinations of
these traits, within a population. Remember, a population refers to a
group of organisms from the same species living in the same area,
such that they are able to interbreed.
Greater genetic diversity is advantageous because it means that a
population is more likely to be able to adapt in response to change.
This is because there is a higher chance that at least some individuals in
the population will have features that enable them to survive and
reproduce.
Let’s consider a classic example: the Galapagos finches. The ancestors
of these finches were initially found on the mainland of South America.
At this point, there was an abundance of different food sources which
included insects, fruits and seeds. As a result, no beak type was
particularly beneficial and there was high genetic diversity.
However, over time, pieces of the mainland broke off to form islands:
carrying all of the flora and fauna out to sea with them! As the islands
moved to different locations, the types of food available on each island
became more uniform. Some islands were mostly seed-based, whilst
others had lots of insects, for example. These differences in food source
acted as a selection pressure, as finches with particular beak types
which enabled them to access the main food source on their island were
more likely to survive. So on islands dominated by seeds, the finches
with thick, sturdy beaks were able to break up the seeds, eat them and
survive.
Over time, the individuals in the population with beneficial traits
were selected for, whilst those with unfavourable traits died out.
As a result, different finch species evolved on each island.
So overall, sexual reproduction is advantageous because it means that a
population is better able to adapt and survive in response to
changes in the environment.
Disadvantages

However, sexual reproduction costs the parents time, energy and
resources, which could be used to promote their own survival! The
process of finding and competing for a mate is usually a pretty
demanding task. Not to mention that the process of mating itself can
make individuals vulnerable to catching sexually transmitted
diseases. Yikes.
Plus, many organisms which sexually reproduce often invest an even
greater amount of resources into their offspring, in order to care and
protect them. Although this increases the offspring’s chances of
survival, it also means that organisms which sexually reproduce often
have a slower reproductive rate. This is because the parents’
resources are limited so that they can only support a certain number of
offspring at any one time.
Asexual Reproduction
Asexual reproduction, on the other hand, involves a single parent,
which produces a new individual from part of itself. Types of
asexual reproduction include binary fission, budding, spores, cuttings
and graftings. It produces offspring that are genetically identical to
each other and to the parent.
Advantages
Compared to sexual reproduction, asexual reproduction is much more
efficient. This is because the amount of time and energy needed to
produce offspring is comparatively a lot less! For example, the parent
doesn’t need to spend time finding or competing for a mate.
Overall, asexual reproduction is advantageous because it means that a
population is able to increase in size very quickly, especially in a
stable environment. Why? Well, if the environment is unchanging, all
of the organisms will have already evolved characteristics which allow
them to grow optimally in this environment.
Disadvantages

The key disadvantage of asexual reproduction is the complete opposite
of the advantage of sexual reproduction: it results in populations with
low genetic diversity. A population with low diversity means that all of
the organisms are more similar.
This makes them all equally vulnerable to environmental factors,
infections and pests so that a change in the environment could easily
wipe out the whole population. This is because there is a lower
chance that the population will have individuals who are capable of
surviving in the new environment, and evolution cannot occur.
The Irish Potato Famine is a common example here. During the mid-
1800s, potatoes were the sole food source for a third of the Irish
population. These potato plants had very low genetic diversity because
they basically all originated from cuttings of the same plant. Chaos
struck when the nation was hit with a new fungal disease, which totally
wiped out all of the potato plants and caused widespread starvation.

• Sexual reproduction ensures the continuation of a species through the production of
genetically varied offspring.
• Genetic variation increases survival chances in changing environments.
• Mammals maximise reproductive success through:
o Internal fertilisation – increases chance gametes meet.
o Implantation – embryo attaches to uterine wall for protection and nourishment.
o Pregnancy – internal development of embryo ensures survival.
• Reproductive processes are regulated by hormones for correct timing and coordination.
Fertilisation
• Definition: The fusion of a male (sperm) and female (egg) gamete to form a diploid zygote.
• Location: Oviduct (fallopian tube).
• Process:
o Sperm swim toward egg, attracted by oviduct fluid and chemical signals.
o
Progesterone + alkaline pH mature sperm → hypermobile.
o Sperm must penetrate three protective layers of the egg.
o Only one sperm enters – others are blocked (prevents polyspermy).
o Fusion of nuclei → zygote (2n) forms.
o Zygote begins cleavage divisions as it moves toward the uterus.
Implantation
• Definition: The attachment of the blastocyst (early embryo) to the endometrium of the
uterus.
• Process:
o Occurs about 6–7 days after fertilisation.
o The outer cells of the blastocyst form the placenta.
o The inner cell mass develops into the embryo.
o Marks the beginning of pregnancy.
Pregnancy (Gestation)
• Definition: Period during which one or more offspring develop inside the uterus.
• Hormonal Control:
o hCG (human chorionic gonadotropin): maintains corpus luteum early in pregnancy.
o Progesterone: thickens and maintains endometrium; inhibits uterine contractions.
o Oestrogen: stimulates uterine growth and blood supply.
o Oxytocin: triggers uterine contractions at birth.

• Corpus luteum: Active for first 3 months → then degenerates.
• Placenta: Takes over hormone production and nutrient/gas exchange after ~12 weeks.
• Ovarian cycle resumes only after breastfeeding ends.

Reproductive Technologies

Humans have always had a particular knack for playing around with different processes in
order to get what they want. From using yeasts to brew beer to capturing sunlight as a
source of energy, our ability to exploit natural processes is both diverse and widespread.
Unsurprisingly, humans have also found ways to intervene with reproduction, and believe it
or not, we have justifiable motives for doing this! As you may have guessed, we refer to
these intervention techniques as reproductive technologies.

Types of Reproductive Technologies
Artificial Insemination
Artificial insemination involves deliberately introducing male sperm into the female
reproductive tract, by a method other than sexual intercourse, which is why we say it's
""artificial"". The process involves collecting sperm from a male, which may be used
immediately or may be frozen and stored before being inserted into the reproductive tract
of a fertile female.
Artificial Pollination
Artificial pollination is basically just the plant version of artificial insemination. That is, it
refers to when humans manually facilitate the natural plant pollination process. This
involves the transfer of pollen from the stamen (male part) of one flower to the stigma
(female part) of another flower.
There are two methods for performing artificial pollination:
• Mechanical pollination
• Hand pollination
In mechanical pollination, large amounts of pollen are released from aeroplanes or blowers
directly onto the plants. Although this is relatively quick and doesn’t require much labour,
not many flowers are successfully pollinated, meaning it’s pretty inaccurate.
In hand pollination, a person uses a small brush to transfer pollen between flowers. This is
obviously super labour intensive, but it has a much higher accuracy rate than mechanical
pollination.
Positive Outcomes of Reproductive Technologies
Artificial insemination and artificial pollination can be used to positive effect in:
• Producing offspring with desirable characteristics.
• Overcoming geographical barriers between organisms.
• Improving the rate of reproductive success.
Offspring with Desirable Characteristics

Let's look at an animal example. So semen from a ram which is prized for high-quality wool
may be purchased by a farmer. The farmer can then use this sperm to impregnate his own
ewes so that they produce offspring with higher quality wool. This wool can be sold for
greater profits, increasing the farmer's income.
In plants, artificial pollination may be used to breed crops with higher yields, larger fruits
and/or disease resistance, to name a few examples.
Overcoming Geographical Barriers
The ability to transport semen and pollen over large distances means that genes can be
spread across the world, increasing biodiversity. This is particularly crucial to wildlife
conservation projects where it may not be possible to transport individuals over long
distances, just to... you know, get it on.
For example, pandas are notoriously difficult to breed in captivity because they rarely mate
and the females are fertile for a relatively short amount of time. In this case, artificial
insemination using sperm transported from overseas has played a huge role in preventing
panda extinction.
Improving Reproduction Rates
A good example of this is how artificial insemination can be used in humans to improve the
chances of a successful pregnancy. This technology is used when a male or female has
fertility issues (because they’re unable to have sex, the male has a low sperm count, or the
female has endometriosis, for example), or if donor sperm is being used.
As for artificial pollination, an example is apple trees, which must be pollinated by another
individual from the same species (rather than self-pollinating, like other trees). Waiting for
the right pollen to be transferred by the wind or a pollinator can take a long time. This is
particularly true considering the decline of natural pollinators like bees. Save the bees,
everyone! So artificial pollination can quickly achieve the same results as natural pollination,
which ultimately reduces harvest times.
Summary of Positive Outcomes, By Application
Overall, we can see that reproductive technologies have important roles in many fields. To
quickly sum that up in terms of each specific application:
• In agriculture, both artificial insemination and artificial pollination are used to
produce crops and livestock with characteristics that lead to increased profits, at a
faster rate.

• In wildlife conservation and captive breeding programs, artificial insemination and
artificial pollination can be used to improve the reproductive success of endangered
species.
• In human reproduction, artificial insemination can be used to improve the chances
of a successful pregnancy.
Negative Outcomes of Reproductive Technologies
When used excessively, artificial insemination and pollination can lead to reduced
biodiversity since only a few individuals are allowed to contribute to the next generation.
Over time, "desirable" traits become more and more common, whilst "undesirable" traits
are bred out.
This means that the population is:
• Less likely to survive sudden environmental changes, and
• More likely to suffer from inbreeding.
Reduced Resilience to Sudden Changes
Now, if you remember anything about evolution, you’ll know that biodiversity is crucial if a
species is to survive environmental change. This is because a species with greater
biodiversity is more likely to contain at least some individuals with features that enable them
to survive in the new conditions.
The problem with reproductive technologies is that they’re often used to breed animals or
plants with the same, "desirable" characteristics.
For example, a farmer will want all of their cattle to be large and produce lots of milk so they
can make more money per cow. But if all of the cows are the same, then a disease which
affects one of them is more likely to affect all of them and totally wipe out the population.
Inbreeding
With increased inbreeding, it’s more likely that less favourable genes (such as those which
are responsible for recessive genetic diseases) will show up more frequently in the
phenotype. This all comes down to genetics, although we don't need to go into this in depth
right now!

Inquiry Question:
How important is it for genetic material to be replicated exactly?
Purpose of Mitosis
Mitosis is the process of nuclear division that produces two genetically identical daughter cells.
It is vital for:
• Growth – increases the number of cells in an organism.
• Repair – replaces damaged or dead cells.
• Asexual reproduction – produces identical offspring in some plants and simple organisms.
• Genetic stability – ensures that all cells contain the same DNA as the parent cell.
Why Exact DNA Replication Is Important
• DNA carries the genetic code that controls all cell structure and function.
• Each new cell must receive a complete, identical set of DNA to function properly.
• Errors (mutations) during DNA replication can lead to:
o Abnormal cell function or disease (e.g. cancer)
o Developmental problems or genetic disorders
• Accurate replication maintains species stability and proper cell function.

Where Mitosis Occurs
• Growing tissues – bone marrow, skin, hair follicles, root tips.
• Repair sites – after injury or cell death.
• Reproductive tissues in asexual organisms (e.g. budding, regeneration).
Difference Between Cell Division and Mitosis
• Cell division = entire process (nucleus + cytoplasm divide).
• Mitosis = division of the nucleus only.
• Cytokinesis = division of the cytoplasm following mitosis.
Structure of Genetic Material
• Chromatin – DNA + protein in non-dividing cells (uncoiled).
• Chromosome – coiled DNA visible during division.
• Chromatid – one of two identical copies of a duplicated chromosome.
• Centromere – region where sister chromatids are joined and spindle fibres attach.
Phases of Mitosis
Stage Description Key Events
Prophase Chromatin coils into chromosomes. Nuclear membrane breaks down, spindle
forms.
Metaphase Chromosomes line up at the Spindle fibres attach to centromeres.
equator.
Anaphase Sister chromatids separate. Spindle pulls chromatids to opposite poles.
Telophase Two new nuclei form. Chromosomes uncoil, nuclear membranes
reform.
Cytokinesis (After mitosis) Cytoplasm divides, forming two identical cells.
Outcome of Mitosis
• Two identical diploid (2n) cells are formed.
• Each daughter cell has the same chromosome number and DNA as the parent cell.
• This ensures genetic continuity between generations of cells.
Importance of Mitosis
• Growth: Builds multicellular organisms from a fertilised egg.
• Repair: Replaces damaged or old cells.
• Asexual reproduction: Produces clones in some organisms.
• Genetic stability: Ensures all body cells have identical genetic information.

Modelling Cell Replication
1. Overview
• Cell replication ensures continuity of genetic material across generations of cells and
organisms.
• Two main processes:
1. Mitosis – produces 2 genetically identical daughter cells for growth, repair, and
asexual reproduction.
2. Meiosis – produces 4 genetically unique gametes for sexual reproduction.
• DNA replication underpins both processes. It is semi-conservative and follows Watson-Crick
base-pairing rules.
2. DNA Replication (Watson & Crick model)
• Structure: Double helix of nucleotides (phosphate, deoxyribose sugar, nitrogenous base).
• Base Pairing: A-T (2 hydrogen bonds), C-G (3 hydrogen bonds)
• Process:
1. Unwinding: DNA helicase separates strands.
2. Base Pairing: Free nucleotides pair with exposed bases.
3. Bonding: DNA polymerase forms phosphodiester bonds between nucleotides.
4. Result: Two semi-conservative DNA molecules (each with 1 old + 1 new strand).
Diagram idea: Label strands, bases, hydrogen bonds, and directionality (5’ → 3’).
3. Mitosis
Purpose: Growth, repair, asexual reproduction
Outcome: 2 identical diploid daughter cells
Phases & Key Features:
1. Interphase: DNA replicates; cell prepares for division
2. Prophase: Chromosomes condense; spindle forms

3. Metaphase: Chromosomes align at the metaphase plate
4. Anaphase: Sister chromatids separate to opposite poles
5. Telophase: Nuclear membrane reforms; chromosomes decondense
6. Cytokinesis: Cytoplasm divides, producing 2 identical cells
Diagram idea: Show replicated chromosomes (X-shaped), spindle fibres, and separation of sister
chromatids.
4. Meiosis
Purpose: Sexual reproduction; increases genetic diversity
Outcome: 4 genetically unique haploid cells
Two Divisions:
• Meiosis I (Reduction division): Homologous chromosomes separate → 2 haploid cells
• Meiosis II (Equational division): Sister chromatids separate → 4 haploid cells
Key Features:
• Crossing Over (Prophase I): Exchange of alleles between homologous chromosomes →
increases genetic diversity
• Independent Assortment (Metaphase I): Homologous chromosomes segregate
independently → random combination of maternal and paternal chromosomes
• Effect on Gametes: Segregation and crossing over together produce a wide variety of
genetically unique gametes
Diagram idea: Show homologous chromosomes pairing (bivalents), crossing over, and separation
stages.

• Clearly label:

o Haploid vs diploid
• Use arrows and colour coding to show movement and separation.

Meiosis – Notes (Year 12 Biology, 5.2.1 & 5.2.2)
Meiosis is the type of cell division that produces gametes (sex cells) — sperm and eggs — each
containing half the number of chromosomes of the parent cell. It ensures genetic variation and is
essential for sexual reproduction.
Purpose of Meiosis
• To produce haploid cells (n) from a diploid cell (2n).
• To ensure genetic diversity in offspring.
• To maintain a constant chromosome number across generations.
Key Features
• Occurs in gonads (ovaries & testes).

• One DNA replication, two divisions:
o Meiosis I (reduction division) → chromosome number halves.
o Meiosis II (equational division) → separates sister chromatids.
• Produces four genetically unique daughter cells.
Stages of Meiosis
MEIOSIS I – Reduction Division
1. Prophase I
• Chromosomes condense.
• Homologous chromosomes pair up (synapsis).
• Crossing over occurs → DNA is exchanged between chromatids → genetic variation.
• Nuclear membrane dissolves; spindle forms.
2. Metaphase I
• Homologous pairs line up at the equator.
• Independent assortment: random orientation of maternal/paternal chromosomes →
genetic variation.
3. Anaphase I
• Homologous chromosomes separate to opposite poles.
• Sister chromatids remain attached.
4. Telophase I & Cytokinesis
• Two haploid cells form.
• Chromosomes still duplicated (X-shaped).
MEIOSIS II – Equational Division
(No DNA replication before this stage)
1. Prophase II
• New spindle forms in each haploid cell.
2. Metaphase II
• Chromosomes line up at the equator (like mitosis).
3. Anaphase II
• Sister chromatids separate and move to opposite poles.
4. Telophase II & Cytokinesis
• Nuclear membranes reform.
• Cytoplasm divides.
• Four non-identical haploid gametes are produced.
Importance of Meiosis for Continuity of Species
(5.2.2)
1. Maintains Chromosome Number
• Halves chromosome number (n), ensuring that at fertilisation:
o n + n → 2n, restoring the diploid number.
• Prevents doubling of chromosomes each generation.
2. Creates Genetic Variation

Genetic variation is critical for survival, adaptation, and evolution.
Meiosis creates variation through:
• Crossing over (Prophase I) → new allele combinations.
• Independent assortment (Metaphase I) → random mix of maternal & paternal
chromosomes.
• Random fertilisation → any sperm can fertilise any egg.
Greater variation = higher chance individuals will survive environmental change → species
continuity.
3. Allows Natural Selection to Act
• Variation leads to different phenotypes.
• Beneficial traits → more likely to survive & reproduce.
• Ensures long-term survival of species.

Purpose of DNA Replication
• Ensures genetic material is copied exactly before cell division (mitosis or meiosis).
• Produces two identical DNA molecules so each daughter cell receives the same
genetic information.
• Occurs during S phase of interphase.
Watson & Crick Model – Structural Features
DNA Structure
• Double helix (twisted ladder).
• Made of nucleotides, each with:
o Phosphate group
o Deoxyribose sugar
o Nitrogenous base (A, T, C, G)
Base Pairing Rules (Complementary Pairing)
• Adenine (A) pairs with Thymine (T) → 2 hydrogen bonds
• Cytosine (C) pairs with Guanine (G) → 3 hydrogen bonds
• Complementary base pairing is essential for accurate replication.
Three-Step Process of DNA Replication
1. Unwinding (Initiation)
• DNA Helicase breaks the hydrogen bonds between bases.
• Two strands separate → forms a replication fork.
• Each separated strand acts as a template for building a new one.
2. Adding New Nucleotides (Elongation)
• DNA Polymerase adds complementary nucleotides to each template strand:
o
A → T
o
T → A
o
C → G
o
G → C
• Polymerase can only add nucleotides in the 5’ → 3’ direction.
o Leads to:
• Leading strand → continuous replication
• Lagging strand → discontinuous replication in Okazaki fragments
3. Joining (Termination)
• DNA ligase seals the Okazaki fragments on the lagging strand.
• Two identical double helices form.
• Each molecule contains:
o One original (parent) strand
o One newly synthesised strand
This is called semi-conservative replication.

Semi-Conservative Replication
• Proposed by Watson & Crick, proven by Meselson–Stahl.
• Each new DNA molecule conserves half of the original parent DNA strand.
• Reduces errors and increases stability of genetic information.

DNA Replication – Breakdown and Formation of
Chemical Bonds
1. Breaking of Bonds
• DNA helicase unwinds the double helix.
• It breaks the hydrogen bonds between complementary bases (A–T, C–G).
• This separates the two strands and exposes the template strands for replication.
2. Formation of New Bonds
• Free nucleotides in the nucleus pair with exposed bases on each template strand.
• Hydrogen bonds reform between complementary bases:
o A pairs with T (2 hydrogen bonds)
o C pairs with G (3 hydrogen bonds)
3. Formation of the Sugar–Phosphate Backbone
• DNA polymerase joins new nucleotides together.
• It forms phosphodiester bonds between the sugar of one nucleotide and the
phosphate of the next.
• This creates a strong sugar–phosphate backbone on each newly synthesised strand.
4. Result
• Two identical DNA molecules are formed.
• Each consists of:
o One original (parent) strand

o One new (daughter) strand
• This is called semi-conservative replication.

We know that everything about a person ultimately comes down to
their DNA. But, if we’re going to explain how this works in more depth
later on, we’re going to need to be familiar with some more specific,
scientific terms!
In this lesson, we’ll look at:
1. Defining 'genotype' and some examples
2. And, defining 'phenotype' and some examples.
Heads up, as we're only going to focus on definitions and examples here,
the explanations of the genetics will come a little later as we move
through the course.
Let’s get straight into it!
Defining 'Genotype'

'Genotype' refers to an organism’s genetic makeup for a particular
characteristic. That is, it's the set of alleles in an organism’s DNA which
code for a trait.
Typically, an individual's genotype for a trait consists of two alleles:
one inherited from their mother, and one from their father.
In humans, genotype is determined at fertilisation, when a sperm and
egg fuse to form the first cell of a person, which we refer to as a zygote.
Examples
So there are three alleles for blood type, referred to as ‘I ’, ‘I ’ and ‘i’. If a
A B
person inherited an I allele for blood type from their mother and an ‘i’
A
allele for blood type from their father, we would say that their genotype
for blood type is ‘I
A
i’.
When it comes to eye colour, in simple terms there are three alleles:
brown, blue and green. If a person inherited a brown allele (‘B’) from
their mother and a blue allele (‘b’) from their father, their genotype for
eye colour would be ‘Bb’.
We’ll look at the meaning of these coded letters in more depth when we
go through inheritance later on. For now, just keep in mind that we
represent genotype using letters.
Defining 'Phenotype'
'Phenotype' refers to an organism’s physical expression of a
particular characteristic. That is, the observable or expressed
characteristics of an organism.
An organism’s phenotype for a particular characteristic is determined
by two things: which alleles are present (the genotype) as well as
environmental factors.

The relative influence of genotype and environmental factors varies
depending on the characteristic. So, the phenotype of some traits is
entirely controlled by the genotype, whereas the phenotype of other
traits is affected by genes and certain environmental factors.
Examples
For example, a person’s blood type (that is, the observed phenotype) is
determined entirely by genotype. The phenotype of a person with the
genotype I i would be that they have blood type A (again, we’ll cover the
A
inheritance behind this later on).
In comparison, skin colour is determined by both genotype and
environmental factors. Skin colour primarily depends on how much of
a pigment, called melanin, is present in the skin. Melanin’s job is to
absorb excess UV rays from the Sun, that would otherwise cause your
skin to degrade and develop cancer. We all have genes which code for
the production of melanin in our skin, although some people naturally
have greater amounts of melanin than others. However, when exposed
to excess UV rays (from the Sun), your skin will produce more melanin
to try and protect you. This results in a change in your phenotype, as
your skin appears darker.
Identical twins are another great example of how the environment can
influence phenotype. Identical twins are formed from the same zygote:
that is, all of their cells originated from the same cell. This means that
they have exactly the same genotype for every trait. However, identical
twins often look increasingly different as they grow up. This comes
down to lots of different environmental factors. Diet is the most
straightforward example: if one twin ate lots more than the other, then
we would observe that this twin was heavier than the other.
So, don’t worry, our genes don’t completely determine our destiny!
Key Takeaways
• Genotype refers to an organism’s genetic makeup for a particular
characteristic.

• That is, it's the alleles for a trait.
• The alleles which make up a genotype are typically represented
using letters.
• For example, a person’s genotype for blood type might be IAi.
• Phenotype refers to an organism’s physical expression of a
particular characteristic.
• That is, it's how the trait appears.
• The phenotype of some traits is entirely controlled by the
genotype. For example, a person with the alleles for type A blood
(IAi or IAIA) will have blood type A.
• The phenotype of other traits is determined by the genotype and
certain environmental factors. For example, skin colour is
determined by a person’s genetic predisposition to produce
melanin, as well as Sun exposure.

• Determines if a person has or is at risk of a genetic disease.

DNA Profiling – Key Points
Purpose:
• DNA profiling is used to identify individuals and study inheritance patterns.
• Based on unique DNA sequences such as Short Tandem Repeats (STRs).
Steps to Construct a DNA Profile:
1. DNA Extraction:
o DNA is collected from cells (blood, saliva, hair).
2. DNA Amplification:

o PCR is used to amplify specific regions containing STRs.
3. Separation of DNA Fragments:
o Gel electrophoresis: DNA fragments are separated by size in a gel.
o Capillary electrophoresis: Automated, uses fluorescent tags to separate and detect
fragments.
4. Analysis:
o DNA fragments create a pattern of bands (gel) or peaks (capillary electrophoresis).
o STR patterns are compared between individuals to determine relatedness or
identity.
Applications:
• Forensics: Identify suspects or victims using DNA from a crime scene.
• Paternity testing: Determine biological parentage.
• Population genetics: Study inheritance patterns and genetic diversity in populations.
Key Features:
• STRs vary in length between individuals, providing a unique genetic fingerprint.
• Highly accurate, especially when multiple STR regions are analysed.

Introduction to Disease – Key Points
Definition of Disease:
• A disease is any condition that impairs normal structure or function of an organism.
• Can affect specific organs, tissues, or the whole body.
Two Main Types of Disease:
1. Infectious Disease:
o Caused by pathogens such as bacteria, viruses, fungi, or parasites.
o Can be transmitted between individuals or through contaminated food, water, or
vectors.
o Examples: Influenza, tuberculosis, malaria.
2. Non-Infectious Disease:
o Not caused by pathogens and cannot be transmitted between individuals.
o Often caused by genetic factors, lifestyle, or environmental influences.
o Examples: Diabetes, cancer, heart disease.
Additional Notes:
• Understanding disease types helps in diagnosis, treatment, and prevention.
• Provides background for studying genetic disorders and biotechnological applications in
medicine.
