What are Mutations and Mutagens?

A mutation is a change in the genetic code (DNA structure), which alters the sequence of
nucleotides, they can occur spontaneously due to errors in meiosis or mitosis, natural
degradation over time, or induced from environmental agents known as mutagens.  A
mutagen is any environmental agent that has the potential to cause a mutation.  Mutagenesis
is the process of mutations occurring, that change from the normal base pairs to altered base
pairs.  A carcinogen can cause cancer (mutations in genes that aid the repair of DNA)

Physical Mutagens (electromagnetic radiation sources)

Main sources of radiation are from the higher end of the spectrum: UV rays, X rays and
gamma rays.  X rays and gamma rays are dangerous because they have ionising radiation
(ability to strip electrons from atoms) which causes damage to DNA and produces free
radicals (reactive molecules).

UV radiation is non-ionising but penetrates your skin and has enough energy to hit DNA.
Because it is non-ionising it can cause a thymine dimer (when 2 thymine nucleotides next to
each other to bond together and cause a bulge in DNA).  This is a problem when copying DNA
or making an amino acid using polypeptide synthesis, and it can also lead to cancer.

Chemical Mutagens

Chemical interact with DNA by tricking the DNA into thinking they’re a part of it, by
changing the nucleotides or directly getting in between nucleotides.  This causes misreading
and changes in polypeptide synthesis.  Chemical mutagens are often ingested (like alcohol
and tobacco) or environmental (like cleaning products, asbestos, lead).

Chemical mutagens include: Intercalating agents (slip between two base pairs and alter DNA
form at that point), base analogue (has a similar form to nitrogen bases, replication of these
molecules is implemented instead of original bases) and reacting chemicals (react directly
with nitrogen bases and chemically modify them).  Metals like mercury can cause
mutagenesis if individuals, like miners, are exposed to it for prolonged periods of time.

Naturally occurring mutagens (biological mutagens)

Occur at normal levels in the environment and may cause mutations if individuals have
prolonged exposer to it, they are living molecules which can cause mutation and cancer.
Living viruses replace our DNA with their own, leading to incorrect polypeptide synthesis,
bacteria and fungi release toxins that cause free radicals (unstable atoms) that damage cells
by knocking DNA out of place.

Examples of point mutations

Sickle cell anaemia

Sickle cell disease is a condition that involves two alleles: the normal allele and the mutant,
sickle allele.  The normal alleles DNA codes for the protein Glu, whereas the mutated sickle
DNA has a base of adenine instead of thymine which results the protein Val.  The normal
allele is required to produce normal haemoglobin proteins, which result in normal red blood
cells.  Individuals who are homozygous with the nutant allele have abnormal haemoglobin
that leads to sickle shaped red blood cells the poor oxygen transport.  However, heterozygous
individuals produce both normal and abnormal haemoglobin.  These ‘carriers’ have a unique
benefit from their one mutant allele – they are less likely to catch malaria, as this infectious
parasite dies inside sickle cells.

Double-muscled cattle

Belgian Blue cattle have a distinctive trait known as ‘double-muscle’ as they produce higher
muscle mass than other breeds, and thus higher quantities of lean meat.  This trait arose in
cattle from a small deletion within the myostatin gene.  This deletion resulted in both
frameshift and a premature stop codon.  The protein produced from the mutant myostatin
gene is very short and does not function.  The normal myostatin protein promotes muscle cells
to stop growing and so the mutation causes excessive muscle growth.

Example of numerical and structural abnormalities: Down Syndrome

Chromosomal mutations can occur in the form of numerical abnormalities, which involve an
individual having an extra copy of a chromosome or missing a copy of a chromosome.  A
normal human karyotype has 46 chromosomes, which includes 23 pairs of autosomes and 2
sex chromosomes.  The most common numerical abnormality present in live births is down
syndrome (approximately 15 out of 10 000 births).  This condition is also sometimes referred
to as trisomy 21, due to the presence of three copies of chromosome 21.  Down syndrome
causes distinctive physical characteristics, varied levels of intellectual disability and an
increased risk of certain health problems, such as heart disease.  Another type of down
syndrome, called translocation down syndrome, can occur when an extra chromosome 21 is
translocated onto another chromosome, often 13, 14, 15, or 22.

Balanced versus unbalanced abnormalities

Balanced structural abnormalities involve no gain or loss of genes as the genetic material has
only been rearranged, such as inversion and translocation.  However, unbalanced structural
abnormalities involve the gain or loss of genes, such as deletion and duplication.

Crossing over and recombination errors

Crossing over is a process that can occur between homologous chromosomes during meiosis I.
Pairs of homologous chromosomes align at the centre of the cell during meiosis I.  Crossing
over typically involves the two inner chromatids of a pair of homologous chromosomes
exchanging corresponding genes between the maternal and paternal copy.  Crossing over
creates recombinant chromosomes that carry different alleles to the parent chromosomes.
Crossing over is one of the ways that meiosis increases variation in offspring.  If crossing over
does not occur in the typical manner, recombination errors can occur, seen in the following
examples:

Two homologous chromosomes meet in the centre of a cell during meiosis I, however
they do not align correctly which results in the homologous chromosomes exchanging
different amounts of genetic material, causing a duplication or deletion mutation in the
chromosomes.
During meiosis I, errors can occur if a chromosome pairs with a non-homologous
chromosome.  Crossing over between these non-homologous chromosomes results in the
recombination of genetic material containing different genes, which cause translocation
mutations.

A gene pool refers to the combination of all the genes (including alleles) present in a
reproducing population. A large gene pool has extensive genetic diversity and can better
withstand environmental changes.

Gene flow is a term used to refer to changes in allele frequency due to new individuals
entering a population or from individuals exiting a population.  These individuals can alter
the gene pool of the population

Natural selection is a process by which individuals possessing traits that are advantageous for

Sexual selection is a process whereby some traits become more common in a population due to

Effect of Gene Flow on Gene Pool

Gene flow is the transfer of genetic material from one population to another, through
immigration.  As species from two populations mate with each other and exchange genetic
material, the exchange of alleles increases allele frequency in population, hence increasing
genetic variation which results in an increase in gene flow.  Alleles may be lost from the
population through emigration. This effect is also more noticeable in smaller populations.

Effect of Genetic Drift on Gene Pool

Genetic drift is the change in allele frequency due to chance, which causes individuals to be
different, not necessarily more successful.  Since one or more alleles are lost in a population
due to random events, the allele frequency of gene pool will decrease.  The remaining
individuals may not be an accurate representation of the genotype frequencies of the original
population.  The change in allele frequency due to genetic drift is greater if the population is
small.

Bottleneck Effect
A type of genetic drift that occurs when a chance event causes a drastic decrease in
population size, resulting in genetic drift.  The decrease in allele frequency lowers
genetic variation, and thus decreases gene pool.

Founder Effect
A type of genetic drift that occurs when a new population is started by a small number
of individuals called ‘founders’ who are not representative of the original population.
The new population has a different allele frequency compared to the old population, as
founders may not carry all genetic variation of old population, which overall lowers
genetic variation in gene pool compared to the old population.

Using population data in conservation genetics

Conservation genetics is the use of data on the DNA and genes of a population to help guide
management decisions that seek to preserve the population.  Conservation genetic projects
usually focus on endangered species and the development of suitable preservation plans.

Genetic diversity assists in the long-term survival of a population.  When DNA samples from
the population indicate low genetic biodiversity, conservation programs can sometimes
include the introduction of new genetic material to the population.

Recombinant DNA (rDNA)

Recombinant DNA is defined as the DNA that results from moving a section of DNA from its
normal location and inserting it into DNA at another site, which can be within the DNA of the
same organism of a different organism.  Recombinant DNA can occur naturally, or it can be
produced by using biotechnology.

Recombinant DNA for cloning genes in bacteria

Recombinant DNA technology can be used to clone genes in bacteria, this method of gene
cloning involves first creating a transgenic bacterium with the desired gene inserted into its
DNA plasmid, along with the appropriate gene regulators.  When this bacterium undergoes
binary fission to reproduce, more transgenic bacteria with the desired trait are produced.
Hence each offspring bacterium has a clone of the gene and so can produce the gene product.

This method of gene cloning is used for producing human insulin.  The human gene for insulin
and a suitable gene regulatory sequence are inserted into an E. coli bacterium.  This
transgenic bacterium reproduces by binary fission, resulting in multiple bacteria, each
containing a clone of the gene for producing human insulin.  When these transgenic bacteria
are grown under ideal conditions, they quickly produce a large amount of human insulin that
can be harvested and purified for medicinal use.

Transgenic organisms

Transgenic organisms contain foreign DNA (rDNA), and they are created using genetic
engineering to insert one or more foreign genes into an organism’s genome.  Transgenesis
occurs when DNA from a different organism is introduced into a living organism, and as a
result the organism exhibits new traits that are transferred to offspring.  The insertion of
recombinant genes into an organism is possible as the DNA in all organisms has the same
molecular structure, featuring the same bases.  It is important when inserting recombinant
DNA that appropriate gene regulators are included, or the desired gene will not be
transcribed.

Methods of Producing Transgenic Organisms

-  Plasmids: gene editing in bacteria, facilitated by availability of naturally occurring

plasmids into which genes may be easily inserted.

-  Retroviral vectors: retroviruses being exploited to deliver foreign genetic material to a
cell.  They insert RNA into host cells, which then reverse-transcribe them into their
genomes.

-  DNA Microinjection: Gene of interest is injected into a reproductive cell by a fine glass
needle, which is then cultured in vitro until it develops into an embryo.  Then this is
implanted into the uterus.

-  Gene therapy is a new form of medicine, with the potential to replace current

such as golden rice, to address hunger in poor and developing countries and work towards

creators formed a deal with a company to produce it in large quantities for humanitarian

Sumatran rhino (animal biotechnology)

In animal biotechnology, researchers are mapping relatedness between animals (in the wild
and in captivity) and then designing breeding programs to maximise hybrid vigour and
genetic biodiversity. Some species that are close to extinction, such as the Sumatran rhino,
cannot be saved in the wild. Captive breeding technologies are critical in saving these species.
The southern white rhino has been brought back from the brink of extinction by captive-
breeding programs. At the start of the 20th century, there were approximately 50 southern
white rhinos left. As a result of captive breeding with close mapping of relatedness, there are
now over 20 400 white rhinos, with no apparent birth defects despite inbreeding from a small
parent population. The rhinos remain in captive populations in conservation centres, to
reduce the risk of them being eliminated in one place by disease, natural disaster or poaching.
