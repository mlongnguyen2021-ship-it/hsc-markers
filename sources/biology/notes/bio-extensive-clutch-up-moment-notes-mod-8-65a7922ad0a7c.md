Homeostasis - How is an organism’s internal environment maintained in
response to a changing external environment?
Homeostasis - maintenance of a relatively stable internal environment even
when the external environment is changing.
Maintenance of homeostasis involves 2 steps:
1. Detecting stage - involves receptors that detect stimuli
2. Counteracting stage - involves effectors that respond to stimuli and reduce its effects
Stimuli - any internal or external factor/change which can be detected by our body receptors.
E.g. - increase or decrease in body temperature
- Loud sound
- low/high blood sugar levels
- Bright light
Receptors - sensory nerve cells which detect stimuli
Receptor Stimulus Organ Examples
Thermoreceptor Heat Skin, hypothalamus
Chemoreceptor Chemicals (ph, CO2, O2) Olfactory, tongue
Photoreceptor Light Retina
Mechanoreceptor Movement Skin, ears
Integrator (control centre) - hypothalamus & pancreas
Effectors - muscles or glands that carry out a response to the stimulus -> return body back to
homeostasis / reverse the initial effect of the stimulus
E.g. Iris in your eye shrinks the pupil if light is bright
Negative feedback loop - mechanisms which detect the change and induce a response which
counteracts the change and returns the body to homeostasis / stable state.
Detect change -> process change -> decide on response -> returning back to set value
Positive feedback loop - mechanisms which
detect the change and induce a response which causes additional change in the same direction.
E.g. The production of oxytocin results in the production of more oxytocin.

structural and physiological adaptations in endotherms that assist in maintaining
homeostasis
Endotherms (warm blooded) - Organisms that use internal processes to regulate body
temperature in a narrow range, irrespective of the external temperature. They use the heat
produced from their metabolism to help maintain their body temperatures. E.g. mammals and
birds.
Ectotherms (cold blooded)- organisms that have approximately the same body temperature as the
ambient temperature. They have a limited ability to maintain their body temperature at the one level, as it
fluctuates according to the surrounding environment. E.g. plants, invertebrates, amphibians, reptiles and
fish.

– mechanisms in plants that allow water balance to be maintained
Plants must regulate their water salt balance as plants need water to photosynthesise. They
maintain osmotic balance by closing or opening the stomate.
Stress due to lack of water
1. Cells produce abscisic acid
2. Acid is a stimulus detected by guard cells

3. Guard cells move potassium out of the vacuoles
4. Water leaves the guard cells due to osmosis
5. Guard cells close, reducing evaporative loss of water from stomate opening
Too much water
1. Potassium ions move into vacuole
2. Water follows the potassium ions into the vacuole due to osmosis
3. Guard cells expand and swell causing them to open
4. An opened guard cell allows water to evaporate from the plant through opening the
stomate
Other mechanisms / adaptations
- Orientation of leaves e.g away from sunlight or vertically hanging/ curling of leaves to
reduce sun exposure, which will lead to less water loss. (stomata on inner side of curl,
increasing humidity around stomata, reducing rate of water evaporation)
- Reduced leaf size to reduce the amount of water lost through transpiration
- Fleshy stems and leaves swell to store more water
- Waxy cuticles prevent evaporation
- Sunken stomata
E.g. the Eucalyptus tree has vertically hanging leaves, fewer stomata, waxy cuticles

Lung Cancer Epidemiological Study - a study conducted in Queensland to test the hypothesis that the prevalence of
skin cancer is greater in outdoor workers than indoor workers.
Control: indoor workers
- Done in this specific town were the prevalence was already high
- Picked people randomly from the electoral roll to ensure a broad range of lifestyles are included
- Flaws: people who participate may be of lower economic groups as they may be unemployed or people of
older age as they have more time or people already concerned about skin cancer
- Tried to compensate flaws by following up non-responders
- Participants were examined by epidemiologist
- Variables that may have influenced the results: age, previous occupation, complexion, freckling, ethnicity,
recreational habit, diet, smoking, alcohol, drugs, other diseases, genetics

No statistically significant correlation between outdoor occupations and incidence of skin cancer. It however did show
correlation between skin cancer and complexion, freckling on the back and cutaneous sun damage. Results may
have been due to outdoor workers more likely wearing hats and sunscreen.
Evaluation: the study did not support the hypothesis but it was still valuable as it established a correlation between
fair skin and damaged skin to high incidences.
How to Evaluate / Create an epidemiological study
- Was the aim of the study achieved ? e.g. cause of disease, correlation in trends,
management strategies tested for effectiveness. Is the data answering the aim
- Hypothesis (....more likely to …. Because ….)
- Was the appropriate type of epidemiological study used (Descriptive, Analytical - case
control or cohort, Experiment)
- Reliability: results were consistent, matched with other studies, peer reviewed, variables
are controlled, accurate forms of measures, trained professional collected the data
- Validity:
- conducted over a long period of time
- very large sample group (10,000 +)
- Collect a range of relevant data from a large group of both affected and unaffected
people (set questionnaires)
- Have participants that represent a broad range of society and lifestyles (participants
should not be eliminated on the basis of their answers as this reduces validity)
- control group (show that independent variable is the cause of the change)
- Data on incidence, prevalence, mortality, morbidity rates
- Identify the possible cause of the disease (determinants of health) and risk factors
- Result in the development of a management plan with strategies to control disease
- Evaluate the effectiveness of control and treatment programs
- Final data should be peer reviewed

– educational programs and campaigns
Melanoma - Slip, Slop, Slop: Was developed by the Cancer Council in Australia. Increased awareness
of the link UV exposure & sunbed tanning to skin cancer. It also informed the public of preventive
behaviours and habits that will reduce the risk of developing skin cancer. Programs targeted children and
young adults but also adults with children. Has changed over the years to also include seek (seeking
shade) and slide (sliding on sunglasses) after further research on the harms of sun. Some campaigns
also changed to emphasis on changing behaviours by showing the consequences as many people chose
to ignore campaigns.
Advantages:
- Melanoma incidences have fallen, preventing tens of thousands of cases
- Reaches a large population through multiple platforms
- Ensures early detection and prevents incidence
- Inspires local campaigns which are better suited to the demographic e.g. no hat no play in
schools
- Tailored to specific target groups e.g. Campaigns are more effective and harder to ignore
E.g. Slip, slop, slap for young children, anti-tanning beds for young women
Limitations:
- Campaigns that target children don’t impact parents who have control over children’s lifestyles
- Campaigns don’t enforce the change and therefore people ignore the information
- Some people may find it hard to stop, don’t have the will power e.g. smoking addicts

– genetic engineering

Preimplantation genetic Testing for deafness: eggs are harvested and collected and then fertilized.
Each embryo is then tested for the mutated gene. Embryos that were free of the gene or were carriers
were retained and the other embryos were destroyed. The retained embryos are implanted and the
offspring born will not develop the genetic disease.
Advantages
- More ethical than testing and aborting foetus
- Cost saving - family and health system don’t have to pay for treatments in the future
- Carriers can be identified and discarded - remove disease from future family generations
- High accuracy
Limitations
- Some people are culturally against IVF
- Discarding embryos is unethical
Genetically Modified Organisms for nutritional diseases: golden rice is a genetically modified rice
species that contains genes from maize and genes from soil bacterium. This allows the rice to produce
beta-carotene, which is used by the body to produce Vitamin A. This prevents blindness and a
compromised immune system.
Advantages
- Provides people in developing countries with a more nutritious diet for a cheap price
- Rice is a staple food in many countries and is easily incorporated into the everyday diet
- Reduces the risk of blindness (caused by lack of vitamin A)
- Seeds are easily replanted
Limitations
- smaller yield being produced compared to normal rice
- Opposition to genetically modified organisms due to culture, ethics, religion
- Can cost more which third world countries cannot afford to produce

Kidney Dialysis
Haemodialysis - Blood is pumped out of the body to an artificial kidney machine and returned to
the body by tubes that deliver the filtered blood. The machine removes the metabolic wastes
that have built up inside the person’s blood rather than the kidney.
1. Patient is connected to the dialysis machine
2. Machine draws out the patients blood and delivers it to the dialyzer machine
3. Inside the dialyzer machine, blood is pumped through a system of tubes which have
semipermeable membranes
4. These tubes are submerged in dialysate (dialysis fluid). Dialysate has the same
concentration as blood plasma but without the metabolic wastes
5. Waste materials / toxins move through the semipermeable membrane out of the blood
into the dialysate fluid.
6. The blood is now cleaned and ready to be delivered back to the patient.
Peritoneal dialysis - The inside lining of your own belly acts as a natural filter. Wastes are taken
out by means of a cleansing fluid called dialysate, which is washed in and out of your belly in
cycles. (happens in the peritoneal cavity)
1. A surgical hollow tube is placed in the lower abdomen
2. Dialysate fluid is pumped into the abdominal cavity through a tube coming from a
vertically hanging bag
3. The bag is rolled up and tucked around the waist. The fluid is left in the abdominal cavity
for the diffusion of waste products to take place

4. Once the process is completed, the bag is lowered and the fluid, which contains the
waste material, is allowed to flow out
