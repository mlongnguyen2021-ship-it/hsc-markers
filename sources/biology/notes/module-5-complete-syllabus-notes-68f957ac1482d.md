Mechanisms of reproduction

●  The process by which organisms replicate themselves
●  This means making a copy, a likeness, and thereby providing for the continued

existence of a species

●  May be asexual (no gametes transfer) or sexual (gametes transfer)

Asexual reproduction

●  Only one parent needed
●  No fusion of sex cells (gametes) - genetic materials don’t mix
●  Offspring are genetically identical clones of the parent
●  Uses mitosis to create parent clones, both in unicellular and multicellular organisms
●  Offspring only inherit the genes of that parent
●  Almost never changes the number of chromosomes
●  Forms include;
-  Fission
-  Budding
-  Fragmentation
-  Vegetative reproduction
-
-  Parthenogenesis

Spore formation (sporogenesis)

Methods of asexual reproduction;
Fission

●  Parent organism is replaced by two daughters as it splits in two
●  Occurs in many single-celled organisms (i.e, bacteria and fungi)

Budding

●  Split resulting in a ‘mother’ and smaller ‘daughter’ offspring
●  Some multicellular organisms that do this include jellyfish and hydra
-  Hydra; buds grow into fully matured individual that breaks away from parent

organism
Vegetative reproduction

●  New individuals formed without seeds or spores
●

I.e, miniaturised plants (plantlets) on specialised leaves, and some produce new
plants out of rhizomes (i.e, strawberries)

●  Can also form bulbs or tubers (i.e, tulip bulbs), and some from cuttings (i.e,

pineapples and potatoes)

Spore formation (sporogenesis)

●  Spores form during their biological life
●  Spores grow into a multicellular organism (called gametophytes in plants) without

a fertilisation event

●  Can be done by fungi and some algae

Fragmentation

●  New organism grows from fragments of the parent
●  Fragment develops into mature, fully-grown individual

●  Seen in animals (sea stars), fungi and plants

Parthenogenesis

●  An unfertilised egg develops into a new individual
●

It occurs naturally in many plants, invertebrates (water fleas, some ants, bees, and
parasitic wasps) and vertebrates (some reptiles and amphibians)

●  Aphids; sexual and asexual reproduction - eggs / live nymphs at different times -
typically parthenogenetic and viviparous - offspring resembles parents except for
size and called virginopara

Sexual Reproduction

●  Gametes are produced in sex organs by meiosis
●  Male gamete (sperm) and female gamete (ovum) = zygote
●  The zygote is not identical to either parent
●  The zygote grows into an embryo and develops into a new individual by repeated

mitosis

Asexual Analysis

●  No gametes
●  Only one parent = identical offspring

Asexual Advantages

●  Quick reproduction means there is no need to find a mate
-  Necessary as plants = immobile
●  Competitive advantage if well-adapted
●  More common among plants in harsh environments, where organisms are so

●

specific there is little benefit in population variation
In these environments, organisms can quickly and effectively reproduce when
favourable conditions arise

●  Selective pressures that make asexual reproduction more effective include:
-  Food/resource shortages - asexual reproduction uses less energy
-

Small mating population/time/other mating constraints

Asexual Disadvantages

●  Little/no variation = vulnerability to sudden environment changes, i.e, drought,

disease, new parasite, or predator
-  May result in the survival of few, if any

Sexual Analysis

●

Involves the meeting of gametes which carry genetic information from parents to
offspring

●  Thus, offspring contain a mix of genes, introducing genetic variation into the

population

Sexual Advantages

●  Genetic diversity
●  Random variations may make offspring better suited to new/changing

environmental conditions

●  May out-compete their parents/other individuals in the population -> gaining a

selective advantage

●  Better chance of survival in the event of environmental change

Sexual Disadvantages

●  Demands more time and energy -> courtship, finding a mate, gamete production

and mating, all occur before reproduction

-  The above process may increase vulnerability to predators
●  This means reproduction is often the first functional process sacrificed during

hardships

●  Species become functionally extinct without mates
●  Populations slow to build in favourable conditions

Asexual reproduction in plants - Structures

●  New individuals arise from portions of the roots, stems, leaves or buds of adult
individuals and are genetically identical to the parent. This type of asexual
reproductive process is called vegetative propagation

●  Some adult plants produce vegetative organs (i.e, bulbs, tubers, rhizomes and

suckers, from which new plants can arise. This is equivalent to cloning adult plants)

Perennating Organs

●  Underground organs (roots, stems, etc.) that have enough food to sustain the plant

in a dormant state between seasons

●  Allows the plant to survive adverse conditions (extreme cold/drought, etc.)
●  Parts of the plant above ground may die, and underground organs develop buds

that grow in favourable conditions

●  Buds on deciduous trees are considered organs of perennation
●  Gives rise to new plants when separated, making it asexual
●  Gardeners exploit this by splitting bulbs, cutting rhizomes or runners, taking

cuttings of shoots from stems to grow new plants

●  Vegetative propagation techniques often used in agriculture - perennial crops;

seedless grapes, watermelons, mangoes

Naturally occurring examples of vegetative propagation

●  Runners; long, thin modified stems that grow along soil surface (i.e., strawberries
and spinifex grass). At each node, leaves and roots are produced so the runner can
be subdivided into new plants

●  Rhizomes; are underground, horizontal modified stems, and are characteristics of
ginger, ferns (i.e., bracken fern) and many grasses. It can give rise to a new shoot at
each node. Gardeners often propagate ferns by splitting rhizomes

●  Suckers (or sprouts); modified roots which give rise to new plants. Trees and

shrubs that sucker (i.e., reeds, wattles, and blackberries) can spread into a vacant
habitat after disturbance. These grow into separate plants if the parent shrub dies.
Allows for rapid regrowth (after bushfire/drought)

●  Apomixis; is a form of reproduction where some plants can produce offspring with
generative tissues without fertilisation or seed production. Tissue may be in gamete
form (i.e., unfertilised ovules) or non-reproductive (i.e., leaf tissue). It is termed
generative tissue because it causes plantlets that can produce asexual seeds. Seen in
plants i.e, kangaroo grass, lemon/orange trees, and dandelions

Fertilisation steps
Similar for all types of mammals

1.  The sperm uses enzymes from the acrosome to dissolve and penetrate the

protective layer (zona pellucida) surrounding the egg to reach the cell membrane
2.  Molecules on the sperm surface bind to receptors (specialised proteins) on the egg’s
cell membrane to ensure that a sperm of the same species fertilises the egg, then the
nucleus of the sperm enters the cytoplasm in the egg cell.

3.  Changes at the surface of the egg occur to prevent the entry of multiple sperm

nuclei into the egg

4.  Fusion of the haploid egg and sperm nuclei results in a diploid zygote cell (the

fertilised egg)

Cleavage (days 1-4)

●  A ball of 16 or more undifferentiated embryonic stem cells

Blastocyst (days 5-8)

●  Mitotic divisions continue, the morula becomes a blastocyst as the cells begin to

Implantation (days 8-9)

●  Outer layer sounds ot finger-like projections into a part of the wall of the uterus

Gastrula (days 9-14)

Stages of Meiosis
Meiosis I

●  Interphase: DNA replication occurs, creating identical sister chromatids

●  Prophase I: Chromosomes condense, homologous chromosomes pair up

●  Metaphase I: Homologous chromosome pairs align at the cell's equator.

Anaphase I: Homologous chromosomes are pulled apart to opposite poles of

●  Telophase I and Cytokinesis: Nuclear membranes reform, chromosomes

decondense, and the cell divides into two haploid daughter cells.

Meiosis II

●  Prophase II: Chromosomes condense again, and spindle fibers form
●  Metaphase II: Sister chromatids line up at the cell’s equator
●  Anaphase II: Sister chromatids are pulled apart to opposite poles
●  Telophase II and Cytokinesis: Nuclear membranes reform, chromosomes
decondense, and the two daughter cells divide, resulting in a total of four
haploid daughter cells (gametes)

●  Autosomal inheritance is the inheritance of genes carried on normal
(autosomal) chromosomes that impacts males and females equally

●  Alleles can interact in a dominance/recessive relationship in which the
dominant allele is expressed in the hybrid (heterozygous) form and the
recessive phenotype is only expressed in the homozygous genotype

●  Sex chromosomes, such as the X and Y chromosomes in humans, determine
the sex of offspring, but the X chromosome also carries some genes which
are expressed more frequently in males that only have one X

●  The pattern of sex-linked inheritance is different in males to female

Co-dominance, incomplete dominance, and multiple alleles

●  Alleles can interact in a co-dominant manner in which both alleles are

expressed (e.g, roan cattle)

●  In incomplete dominance, the heterozygous form has an intermediate or a

blended phenotype, different from either parent

●  Multiple allele is the inheritance of a characteristic in which there are more
than two forms of alleles so there is a great variety of possible phenotypes
and genotypes (i.e, human blood types may be A or B - co-dominant - or O -
recessive to both A and B)

Frequency data of characteristics in populations

●  Genetic diversity is reflected in the frequency of characteristics across a

population

●  Frequences of characteristics are collected, recorded and presented to reveal

data on phenotypes in local populations

-  Observations could include characteristics such as eye colour, hair colour,

tongue-rolling, or attached or detached ear lobes

●  Data is limited because phenotypes do not always indicate genotype
●  Sampling may be insufficient to reflect the whole population gene frequency
●  Allele frequency is a measure of the genetic diversity of a gene pool

Analysing single nucleotides polymorphisms

●  A single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) is an alternative form of an allele

resulting from a single base substitution (mutation) in the DNA which occurs
in at least 1% of the population

●  HbS is a SNP of normal haemoglobin (HbA)
●  The sickle-cell trait is a result of HbS in the heterozygous form
●  Sickle-cell anaemia is the result in the homozygous form
●  90% of human variation in DNA occurs as SNPs are found in non-coding

and coding sections of DNA

DNA sequencing

●  The process of determining the sequence of nucleotide bases in a piece of

DNA

●  Tells scientists the kind of genetic information that is carried in a particular

DNA segment

-  Sequence information can be used to determine which stretches of DNA
contain genes and which stretches carry regulatory instructions, turning

genes on or off

●  Requires breaking the DNA of the genome into many smaller pieces,

sequencing the pieces, and assembling the sequences into a single long
“consensus”

Technologies;

●  Polymerase chain reaction (PCR) (used in combination with sequencing)
●  Sequencing by synthesis (SBS)
●  Sequencing by oligonucleotide ligation and detection (SOLiD)
●  Labelling (i.e, use of fluorescent dyes)
●  Separating by electrophoresis
●  Base detection (i.e, use of dyes or light emission)
●  Examples of use include detection of SNPs as indicators of genetic diversity

and disease risk and in the Human Genome Project

The Maxam-Gilbert Method
1.  Isolate DNA strand
2.  Radioactively label one end of DNA by adding a radioactive phosphorus atom

to the phosphate molecule

3.  Chemicals and conditions are then modified to suit a specific base which

results in the base being removed through the ribose sugar it is attached to
4.  The DNA strand is cleaved (cut) at this site. This occurs at each position of

that specific base in the DNA strand being sequenced

5.  The DNA fragments formed then undergo gel electrophoresis, with the
shorter strands moving further through the gel than the longer strands
6.  When all patterns from the gel electrophoresis are compared, the sequence

of the bases on the DNA strand can then be determined

DNA Profiling

●  DNA profiling = the use of DNA tests to establish identity or relationships
●  Most individuals of the same species have nearly identical DNA, but the

sequence at certain locations (loci) throughout the genome varies among
individuals

-  Variations can be used to distinguish one individual from another
●  In profiling;
-  DNA is taken from a biological sample
-  PCR (polymerase chain reaction) is used to produce copies of STR sequences
●  Examples of use;
-  Determining the probability of identity (such as matching crime scene

evidence to individuals)

-  Or a familial relationship by comparing highly variable parts of the DNA of

individuals

The Process (Electrophoresis separation example)
1.  Obtain a sample of DNA (saliva, epithelial cells)
2.  Extract the DNA - chemicals are added to break open and extract the DNA
3.  Copy DNA - STRs (short tandem repeats) are sections of non-coding DNA

that are repeated

Conservation Management

●  Population genetics investigates factors that influence the change of
frequency of alleles over time and across locations and populations

●  Allele frequency changes with mutations, natural selection, gene drift and

gene flow

●  Single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) are used as an indicator of levels of

genetic diversity and to compare the genetic diversity of species

●  Sample size is important for obtaining reliable data for studying population

genetics

●  Genetic diversity is important for the survival of endangered species and

small or isolated populations

●  Conservation management needs to consider genetic diversity as well as

habitat requirements

●  Tasmanian devils are at risk from a contagious cancer that is thought to be
linked to the low diversity of genes associated with its immune system
-  Devil Facial Tumour Disease (DFTD) -> makes it near impossible for them to

eat and has resulted in the species becoming endangered

Inheritance of a disease or disorder and human evolution

●  Many diseases have both genetic and environmental causes so population
genetics data can help determine the genetic factors that contribute
●  Genome-wide association (GWA) studies have helped to collected and

analyse data on the genetic components of disease

●  The frequency of alleles for sickle-cell anaemia resulting from an SNP that
changes haemoglobin is higher in areas where there is exposure to malaria
as it provides some resistance to the protozoan pathogen

●  In human evolution, mtDNA is used as a molecular clock to determine the

data of the last common ancestor

●  DNA from mitochondria and the nucleus are used to compare modern h
umans with recent ancestors such as Neanderthals and Denisovans
