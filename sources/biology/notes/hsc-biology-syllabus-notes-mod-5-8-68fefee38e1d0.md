Cell Replication
Inquiry Question: How important is it for genetic material to be replicated exactly?
• model the processes involved in cell replication, including but not limited to:
– mitosis and meiosis
Mitosis:
o Interphase: DNA replicates.
o Prophase: the chromosomes condense to become visible and the nuclear envelope
breaks down. The miotic spindle begins to form
o Metaphase: Chromosomes align in the middle of the cell and each centromere of a
chromosome becomes attached to a spindle fibre
o Anaphase: The two sister chromatids of each chromosome move away from each other
towards opposite ends of the cell.
o Telophase: The cell prepares to split in two by forming a nuclear envelope around each
set of chromosomes and the spindle disappears.
o Cytokinesis: divides cytoplasm into two, each with their own nucleus.
Meiosis:
o Prophase 1: DNA condenses, nuclear envelope disintegrates. Synapsis occurs
(Chromosome separate into homologous pairs- tetrads). Crossing over occurs.
o Metaphase 1: Tetrads line up in the middle. Independent assortment-> so various
combinations of paternal and maternal chromosome alignment are possible-> Random
segregation
o Anaphase 1: Tetrads are split apart. No homologous pairs
o Telophase 1: Nuclear membrane form, two daughter cells form.
o Cytokinesis 1: Daughter cells are not identical to each other and have half the original
number of chromosomes
o Metaphase 2: Chromosomes align in the middle
o Anaphase 2: Daughter chromosomes move apart to opposite poles.
o Cytokinesis 2: Four resulting daughter cells are not genetically identical to each other
and have half the original chromosome number.
− DNA replication using the Watson and Crick DNA model, including nucleotide composition,
pairing and bonding
1. Unravelling DNA: Topoisomerases unwind ds DNA strands and relaxes them to prevent
super coiling. Helicase breaks the hydrogen bonds between bases “unzipping” the double
helix into two single strands.
2. Building a new strand: semi-conservative-> when DNA is produced, one of the strands in
each new DNA molecule comes from the old DNA molecule. DNA polymerase attaches free
floating nucleotides to their complementary bases on the exposed strands. However,
nucleotides only attach in a 5 prime to 3 prime directions. The leading strand is the 5 prime
to 3 prime strand and attaches bases without issues. The lagging strand causes bases to
attach the opposite way causing fragments called Okazaki fragments. DNA ligase glues the
fragments together.
3. Forming the DNA backbone: DNA polymerase forms the sugar-phosphate backbone of the
new DNA strand. Ligase creates hydrogen bonds and gyrase winds up the strands.

DNA and Polypeptide Synthesis
Inquiry question: Why is polypeptide synthesis important?
• Model the process of polypeptide synthesis, including:
− Transcription and translation
Transcription: RNA polymerases bind to a part of the DNA called the promoter region and the DNA
unzips only the part of the DNA that contains the gene code needed. One strand is called the
template strand, in which the RNA nucleotides code against, and the other strand is the coding
strand (as it has the same code as the mRNA molecule being coded. The RNA nucleotides code
against the template strand, forming a complementary single-stranded mRNA molecule. However
this is known as the pre-mRNA strand
RNA Processing: Pre-mRNA strands contain coding sequences of nucleotides called exons, and
nucleotides called introns which do not code. Directly after transcription, mRNA is spliced, and
introns are removed by a spliceosome. The mRNA strands leaves the nucleus.
Translation:
Initiation-> occurs when the ribosome move along the mRNA molecules attaching the tRNA
molecules by temporarily pairing the bases of the tRNA anticodons with the complementary triplets
of bases (codons) on the mRNA. First codon is always AUG (start codon ).
Elongation-> More tRNA molecules code, 3 tRNA anti-codons at a time. The amino acids from the
tail end of each tRNA are linked to one another by an enzyme to form a polypeptide chain. Each
amino acid is then spliced off its tRNA carrier. The amino acid chain grow as more anti-codons
attach to the mRNA.
Termination-> When a tRNA reaches a stop codon, no more tRNA anti-codons attach and the
polypeptide chain is processed in the cell and folded to form a protein.
− Assessing the importance of mRNA and tRNA in transcription and translation
mRNA (messenger RNA)
o Single- stranded
o Functions as an intermediate molecule
o Carries information from the DNA in the nucleus to the ribosomes in the cytoplasm
tRNA (transfer RNA)
o Three unpaired bases called an anti-codon
o Attaches to the complementary bases (codons) of the mRNA strand
o The specific sequence of three bases at the anti-codon end determines which amino
acid is being carried by that specific tRNA molecule
rRNA (ribosomal RNA)
o Forms a structural part of ribosomes and is made in the nucleolus

Polypeptide synthesis creates proteins that carry out different functions in the body. In specialised
cells in multicellular organisms, only certain genes are expressed in each cell type. Coded

on’ in the DNA of that cell. This ensures that each cell develops the necessary structures. e.g. skin
tissue, genes for the pigment melanin and the protein keratin are switched on in each cell ensuring
that the cells become skin cells.

amino acids.

Mutation
Inquiry Question: How does mutation introduce new alleles into a population?
o Explain how a range of mutagens operate, including but not limited to:
− Electromagnetic radiation sources
Harmful radiation sources, called ionising radiation (X-ray, gamma ray), has enough energy to
break chemical bonds. This leads to deletions, partial chromosome loss, rearrangements of
sequences, interfering with cell division, gene production and cell metabolism.
− Chemicals
Chemical mutagens are chemicals that cause mutations if cells are exposed to them at high
frequencies or for prolonged periods of time. They affect the function of proteins, disturbing
cellular processes.
Examples of chemical mutagens include
➢ Ingested chemicals: alcohol, tar, nicotine, medications and chemicals in food, food
additives and preservatives
➢ Environmental irritants and poisons: organic solvents, cleaning products, asbestos,
pesticides e.c.t
The effect of these mutagens usually results in the incorrect insertion of nucleotides.
− Naturally occurring mutagens
Viruses: Mutations are caused when viruses insert their genetic information into the
chromosomes of the host cell, causing disruption to normal cell function e.g. HPV is an
oncogenic (cancer causing) virus that causes cervical cancers.
o compare the causes, processes and effects of different types of mutation, including but not
limited to
− point mutation
Involves alterations to one (or more) bases in the DNA, such as bases being mistakenly inserted
in place of another. Point mutations can also involve one or more bases being inserted or
deleted from a DNA strand at a single point. Both insertion and deletion cause a massive impact
as they result in a frameshift, where every codon from that point on is affected, which means
that incorrect amino acids are being created, resulting in non-functional proteins.
e.g. sickle cell anaemia is caused when DNA code is changed from CTC to CAC.
What the DNA is changed to
▪ Silent: no change to the final protein due to the redundancy of a codon-> amino acid table.
▪ Missense: there is a change to the final protein (amino acid) e.g. sickle cell
▪ Nonsense: a STOP codon is added early-> premature termination of sequence.
▪ Neutral: the amino acid changes but to a similar property amino acid.
− chromosomal mutation
Involves changes to entire chromosomes or significant parts of a chromosome. Chromosomal
mutations can also include deletion of an entire chromosome, or sections of a chromosome-
Duplication, Inversion, Deletion, Insertion, Translocation.
e.g. Down Syndrome is a chromosomal mutation when individuals have an additional copy of
chromosome 21 in each of their cells.
Deletion-> Part of chromosome is lost/deleted (reduced number of genes)
Insertion/duplication-> could be part of the same/different chromosome duplicating and
adding on (increased number of genes-> redundancy) e.g. Huntington’s diseases
Inversion-> part of chromosome removed, flipped and reinserted e.g. Haemophilia
Translocation-> moving parts of one chromosome to another non-homologous chromosome.
Changes to chromosome number (Aneuploidy)
− Cause by nondisjunction during meiosis
− Non-disjunction is the failure of homologous chromosomes to separate during cell division
(Meiosis 1- homologous chromosomes separating)
− Outcomes are cells with extra or missing chromosomes
Trisomy: an extra copy of chromosomes is present. Can lead to shorted life-span or
development problems.
e.g. Trisomy 21 meaning three copies of chromosome 21, resulting in down syndrome.

➢ distinguish between somatic mutations and germ-line mutations and their effect on an organism
Somatic mutation: Mutation to body cell
▪ Some somatic mutations are compensated for by surrounding healthy cells. In other
cases, the cells with the somatic mutation die and are replaces through the mitotic division
of nearby healthy cells. However, some somatic mutations impact cells division and can
cause uncontrolled cell replication. These mutations are associated with the growth of
cancerous tumours.
Germ-line mutation: Mutation to gametes
▪ If the mutation occurs in a gamete, then the mutation can be inherited by offspring. These
germ-line mutations can give rise to new variations in a population.
➢ assess the significance of ‘coding’ and ‘non-coding’ DNA segments in the process of mutation
Coding DNA (Exons)-> sequence of DNA that codes for proteins and makes up 1.5% of the human
genome. Mutations can affect this type of sequence, affecting protein production.
Non-coding DNA (Introns)-> Does not provide instructions for making proteins instead, it contains
sequences that act as regulatory elements, determining when and where genes are turned on and
off. A mutation in non-coding DNA can cause a protein to be expressed in the wrong time or can
reduce or eliminate the expression of an important protein when it is needed.
➢ investigate the causes of genetic variation relating to the processes of fertilisation, meiosis and
mutations
Genetic variation occurs due to:
▪ Random fertilisation: random sperm fertilises a random ovum
▪ Random segregation: homologous chromosomes separate randomly
▪ Independent assortment: chromosomes separate into daughter cells independently
(father and mother chromosomes are mixed into daughter cells)
▪ Crossing over: new combinations of alleles so each gamete is unique
Mutations increase the types of alleles present in a population, also increasing genetic
variation that can be detrimental (causing death), neutral (no change) or beneficial
(increase population)

3. Artificial embryo twinning- animal
▪ is fertilised by sperm, forming a cell. This cell develops into a clump of identical

process called enucleation and replaced with nucleus from donor cell. Egg cell us
triggered to divide by an electrical impulse, developing into an embryo. Embryo is
transplanted to surrogate mother who gives birth to a genetically identical copy of
original organism.

Whole organism Social implications of whole organism cloning involve how cloning technologies
cloning affect society. In 1997 when the first mammal, Dolly, the sheep was cloned,

While many researchers and industries benefited from the mass production of

protest against cloning for research and agricultural uses, forming organisations
such as the Compassion in World Farming and the American Anti-vivisection
Society. Specifically, the American Anti-vivisection Society argue that 96-99% of
cloning attempts regularly cause death, deformities or severe health problems.
This devalues animal life by treating them as mere commodities. They primarily
protest to “End Animal Cloning” seeking to keep meat and milk products from
cloned animals and their offspring out of human and animal food supply. Their
“No pet cloning” initiative seeks to protect both animals and people from being
exploited by companies attempting to ‘bring back’ a deceased animal. Whole
organism cloning is also being used for the revival of endangered species. For

example, scientists cloned the endangered black-footed ferret, duplicated from
the genes of an animal that died over 30 years ago. The cloned ferret, named
Elizabeth Ann, is a genetic copy of ferret Willa who died in 1988 and whose
remains were frozen in the early days of DNA technology. The successful
reproduction of a cloned endangered species is a landmark in conservation
genetic research, proving that cloning technology can not only help restore
genetic diversity but also allow for future breeding, opening new possibilities for
species recovery. The social response to cloning for conservationist purposes
has generally been received positively. However, some raise ethical concerns
about cloning being a manipulation of life, and its potential for creating
“designer” animals. Cloning is also seen as a band aid solution rather than a real
approach to biodiversity conservation. Another common argument against the
cloning of endangered animals focuses on the sheer cost of such experiments.
The process is expensive and time-consuming and current cloning techniques
have an average success rate of less than 5%. Instead freezing animals DNA and
cells would give an option to clone animals in the future as a conservation effort.

Anthrax bacillus-> He examined the blood of a sheep that had died from anthrax and identified rod-

Kochs postulates
1) The same micro-organisms must be present in every disease host
2) The micro-organisms must be isolated and cultures in the laboratory and accurately described
and recorded
3) When a sample of the pure culture is inoculated into a healthy host, this host must develop the
same symptoms as the original host
4) The micro-organism must be able to be isolated from the second host and cultured and
identified as the same original species.

Epidemiological Studies
• Descriptive studies
Usually the first of study, providing information about the frequency of the disease, which
section of the population is affected (age, gender, SES ect), geographic location and timeframe.
Allows early hypotheses on cause of disease
• Analytical studies
Used to collect more data to statistically analyse hypotheses to find likely causes of disease.
Uses mortality, morbidity, incidence and prevalence.
Divided into two types:
➔ Cohort studies-> studying two or more similar groups of people who are free of

➔ Case-control-> comparing people with the disease (case) to people without
(control) and compare difference to find cause

allowing testing of new drugs by giving one group the trial drug and the other placebo.
Testing effectiveness of medication and compare to what is already on the market

– loss of kidney function
The kidneys are a part of the excretory system. They have two main functions 1) remove
nitrogenous wastes from the blood 2) Maintain optimal salt and water levels (osmoregulation)
Structure and function
▪ Has 3 main functions: filtration, reabsorption and secretion.
▪ Has 3 main regions: cortex, medulla and pelvis.
▪ The functional unit of kidneys is the nephron. Each kidney
contains millions of these units, extending from the cortex
into the medulla.
Nephron structure\
Nephron-> each nephron consists of a Bowman’s capsule, which is spherical,
2-sided hollow, cuplike structures that contains a spherical network of
capillaries called the glomerulus. Far side of the Bowmans capsule leads into
the proximal tubule, the loop of Henle and the distal tubule, which leads into
the collecting duct.
Bowman’s capsule When blood flows through the glomerulus, the waste products such as
nitrogenous waste products including amino acids and glucose, pass
through the glomerulus and into the bowman’s capsule. This fluid is called
glomerular filtrate and carries into the tubules.
Proximal tubule Carries the glomerular filtrate into the loop of Henle. Water and sodium
chloride passively move through the tubule into interstitial fluid.
Loop of Henle A u-shaped segment that is carried from the proximal tubule. Its thin
descending limb is permeable to water, passively, and the ascending limb is
impermeable to water, but permeable to salt, passively in the inner medulla
and then actively in the outer medulla. It is followed by the distal tubule
Distal tubule Carries the fluid out of the Loop of Henle. Reabsorption of water occurs.
Collecting duct Reabsorption of water occurs. Carries fluids to renal pelvis
Filtration: Under high pressure, blood travels from the
heart via the aorta to the renal artery and into the
kidney.
The renal artery branches into smaller vessels until
millions of capillaries are formed. Each capillary
enters a nephron, where it forms the glomerulus
inside the hollow of each Bowmans capsule.
Filtration is based on size of substances. Substances
that are small enough will pass through the walls of

the glomerulus into the Bowmans capsule such as nitrogenous waste products and water with dissolved
amino acids, glucose and salts (ions).
Once inside the Bowman’s capsule, this fluid is called the glomerular filtrate.
Reabsorption: Returns essential components that have been filtered out of the blood back into the
bloodstream. Such as
- Glucose
- Amino acids
- Some vitamins,
- Minerals
- Hydrogen bicarbonate
- Ions (Na+, K+)
- Water
All solutes that are reabsorbed from the nephrons move by active transport and facilitated diffusion.
Glomerular filtrate also contains relatively high concentration of dissolved urea and other wasters, most
which are not reabsorbed.
Secretion: third process that leads to urine formation. The kidneys are continuously processing an
enormous amount of blood (~125ml of blood per min) to form a small amount of urine.
Involves removal of toxic substances including:
- Urea (via diffusion)
- Uric acid
- Ammonia (via diffusion)
- Hydrogen ions
- Drugs such as penicillin, saccharin and morphine
Antidiuretic hormone (ADH) and Aldosterone-> salt and water are reabsorbed from the nephron
tubules into the bloodstream depending on the bodies needs.
The amount of salt and water reabsorbed is controlled by two hormones 1) Antidiuretic hormone (ADH or
vasopressin) 2) Aldosterone
Antidiuretic hormone (ADH): also called vasopressin, it controls/stimulates the reabsorption of water by
adjusting the permeability of the collected ducts and distal tubules. ADH is made in the hypothalamus
but stored/released by the pituitary. Receptors in the hypothalamus monitor and control the
concentration of blood.
If the body has…
High salt concentration o ADH levels increase
o Collecting ducts and tubules become more permeable to water
o More water reabsorbed
o Concentration return to normal (concentrated urine)
Low salt concentration o Less ADH released
o Collecting ducts and distal tubules less permeable to water
o Less water reabsorbed
o More water passes out with urine
o Concentration return to normal
Aldosterone: Produced and released by the adrenal glands which sit above the kidneys. Controls the
amount of salt in the blood by stimulating the reabsorption of salt in the loop of Henle to regulate salt and
water balance in the kidney.

If the body has…
High salt levels o High blood volume and pressure due to large amounts of water
diffusing back into blood.
o Aldosterone levels decreased
o Less salt reabsorbed, therefore, less water diffusing into blood
o Salt levels decreased, blood volume and pressure decreases
Low salt levels o Low blood volume and pressure due to water diffusing out of
blood
o Levels of aldosterone increased
o More salt reabsorbed, therefore, more water diffusing into blood
o Salt levels increased, blood volume and pressure increases

– visual disorders: spectacles, laser surgery
Spectacles-> frames that hold corrective lenses made of clear, hard plastic. The shape is
determined by the visual disorder that needs correction.
➢ Myopia: can be corrected wearing concave lenses which are thicker towards the outside and
thinner towards the centre. These bend the light rays’ outwards, causing them to diverge
before they reach the eye.
➢ Hyperopia: can be corrected by wearing convex lenses which are thicker towards the centre
and thinner towards the edges. This bends the light inwards, causing them to begin converging
before they reach the eye.
LASIK/ Laser surgery->
1. A thin flap of the cornea is lifted up
2. A laser (high energy light beam) is used to reshape the cornea
3. For Myopia-> curvature of the cornea is decreased
For Hyperopia-> curvature of the cornea is increased
4. The flap of the cornea is repositioned and it rebounds to itself.
– loss of kidney function: dialysis
o People with abnormal functioning kidneys are not able to remove wastes such as urea. They
have to undergo renal dialysis to regulate their blood.
o There are two forms of dialysis:
1. Haemodialysis:
➔ Blood is taken out of the body through a vein and passed into a dialyser, which
is a bundle of hollow fibres (coiled to increase SA so rate of diffusion
increases), made of semi-permeable membrane\e.
➔ The dialyser is submerged in dialysis fluid (dialysate) w hich flows in the
opposite direction to the blood to maintain a concentration gradient to
maximise diffusion. Dialysate has the same concentration as blood plasma,
with the metabolic waste. Because the concentration of metabolic waste is
higher in the blood than in the dialysing fluid, the waste materials move through
the semi-permeable membrane into the dialysing fluid by diffusion.
➔ An anti-clotting agent, heparin, is added to prevent clotting. The cleaned blood
is then returned to the body. Continual replacement of the dialysate is
required.
- Disadvantages:
▪ Renal dialysis must be done 3-4 hours, 2-4 times per week
▪ Time consuming
▪ Require external machine
▪ Side effects-> low bp, vomiting, light-headedness
▪ Only limited amount of waste can be removed from blood
▪ Substances such as sodium phosphate and potassium ions do not diffuse
quickly enough and therefore may accumulate in the blood. Patients have to
follow a specific diet to prevent this.
- Advantages:
▪ Can occur in hospital or in home

▪ Can be done during sleep
2. Peritoneal dialysis
➔ Removes waste products from the blood.
➔ During peritoneal dialysis, a cleansing fluid flows through a tube into part of the
stomach area (abdomen).
➔ The inner lining of the abdomen, known as the peritoneum, acts as a filter and
removes wastes from blood.
➔ After a set amount of time, the fluid with the filtered waste flows out of the
abdomen and is thrown away.
- Disadvantages
▪ Need to be able to set up and attach equipment
▪ Risks include hernias, weight gain, infection at catheter site, weaking of
abdominal muscles
- Advantages
▪ Uninterrupted time, most patients able to continue working
▪ Can be done during sleep
