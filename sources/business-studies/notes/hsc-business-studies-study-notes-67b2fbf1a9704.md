Marketing approaches -
Production:
- Began in 1820s
- ‘If we make it they will buy it’
- Product design based off of demand of mass production rather than customer needs
- E.g.) Henry Ford quick and cheap car production, confident people will buy
Selling:
- Began in 1920s
- Emphasis on selling due to increased competition
- Use of tv, radio and convincing sales representatives
- Still neglecting needs of customers
Marketing:
- Began in 1960s after economic boom post WW2
- Customer needs and wants were actually considered
- Customer at the centre of marketing
- Disposable income for the first time and producers needed to satisfy customers wants
- CSR- pressures for businesses to be socially responsible e.g.) environmentally sustainable.
- Customer Orientation- Collecting information from customers and basing marketing on
customers wants and interests. Begins with a sale.
- Relationship Marketing- Priority on customer retention and prolonged satisfaction.
Customer loyalty = repeat sales. Loyalty program

Types of markets -
✮ Intermediate market- consists of wholesalers and retailers who
purchase finished products and sell them again to make a profit.
✮ Consumer market- Are the markets with which we are most familiar. Each time we go
shopping at the local supermarket, we are part of the consumer market.
✮ Resource market- consists of those individuals or groups that are engaged in all forms of
primary production, including mining, agriculture, forestry and fishing.
✮ Industrial markets- includes industries and businesses that
purchase products to use in the production of other products
✮ Mass market- In mass markets the seller mass produces, mass distributes and mass promotes
one product to all buyers. The business doesn’t target it’s products to a specific group of buyers
✮ Niche market- Aka a concentrated or micro market. E.g.) Magazine types

The state of being dependent upon one another. The marketing concept is a philosophy that states
all sections of the business are involved in satisfying a customer's needs and wants while
achieving the business goals.

Laws specific marketing practises
Deceptive and misleading advertising
♡ Fine print - Important conditions are written in a small-sized print and are therefore difficult to
read.
♡ Tests and surveys - Make unsubstantiated claims e.g ‘9 out of 10 people’ prefer a product when
no survey has been conducted.
♡ Accuracy in labelling - ‘made in Australia’ and ‘product of Australia’ have two
distinct meanings.
♡ Special offer - If they imply that a special offer is available for only a limited
period, when in fact the offer is continuously available.
♡ Bait and switch advertising - Involves advertising a few products at reduced and, therefore,
enticing prices to attract customers. When the advertised products quickly run out, customers are
directed to higher priced items.

Price Discrimination
- The setting of different prices for a product in separate markets possibly because:
The markets are geographically separated, for example city and country prices or there is product
differentiation within the one market, for example different electricity prices for domestic and
business users.

Warranties
- A warranty is a promise by the business to repair or replace faulty products.
In recent years, government legislation has made it necessary for businesses to state, clearly and
simply, the terms and conditions of the warranty. A warranty assures the customer that the
business has confidence in the quality of its product and will repair or replace any faulty items
♡ Refunds and Returns Policy
A business is required by law to offer a refund:
• if the products provided are faulty
• do not match the description or a sample
• fail to do the job they were supposed to do.
There is no obligation to offer a refund if the customer has simply changed their mind, has found
the same product at a cheaper price in another store, or damage has occurred after the purchase
was made.

Price skimming
Occurs when a business changes the highest possible price for the product during the
introduction stage. Consumers are willing to pay a high price for a product's novelty features.
Objective is to recover the costs of research and development iPhone
Price penetration
Opposite to price skimming. When a business changes the lowest price possible for a product.
Objective is to sell a large number of products during the early stages of the life cycle -
discouraging competitors from the market

Legal
The legal framework is the interconnected laws that exist to regulate the process of human resources.
Employment contract
-Legally binding formal agreement between employer and employee
A written contract, however, offers more protection and clarifies key duties, reducing potential
disputes. For a contract to be valid, it must involve consent, intent to form a legal relationship, and
mutual benefits.
Under common law, which is developed by courts, both parties have obligations. Employers must
provide work, pay wages, and comply with industrial relations legislation that protects employees from
discrimination and unfair dismissal. They also have a duty of care to provide a safe working
environment.
Employees are required to obey lawful commands, work with care and skill, and act in good faith, such
as not sharing confidential information with competitors.
Additionally, the Ten National Employment Standards (NES) outline minimum conditions for workers,
covering pay, working hours, and leave entitlements, designed to protect vulnerable employees.

Separation
Involuntary Dismissal – Redundancy
Redundancy may be necessary in cases such as workplace closure, project completion, lack of work,
reduced customer demand, or financial difficulties.
Employers must provide documentary evidence, such as financial statements, to justify redundancies.
Factors like length of service, performance, and voluntary departure should be considered when
selecting staff for retrenchment. Notice and leave entitlements must comply with legislation and
industrial agreements. Managers should consult with staff, offer support, and ensure a smooth
transition.
Employers must confirm termination in writing and comply with award provisions, which often
include procedures for retrenchment, notice, and severance pay. Employers may need to prove that the
redundancy is genuine, no alternative work was available, and redeployment options were discussed.
Involuntary Dismissal – Misconduct or Poor Performance:
Staff can be dismissed for various reasons, with summary dismissal being an immediate action for
serious misconduct, such as theft.
Dismissals must be fair and reasonable, and Fair Work Australia assesses whether the reasons were
justified, properly investigated, and if the employee had a chance to respond.
In cases of poor performance, employers must provide written warnings, offer support to help
improve, and notify employees of the reasons for termination, giving them an opportunity to respond.
Employers must follow these processes before dismissing an employee.
Unfair Dismissal:
Selecting staff for dismissal can be risky and requires awareness of legislation and industrial
agreements. Documentation of processes undertaken is also required to avoid claims of unfair
dismissal.
Fair Work Australia provides these grounds for unfair dismissal claims for employees: as a case where
the dismissal was harsh, unjust, or unreasonable, was not genuine redundancy, and did not follow the
Small Business Fair Dismissal Code.
Disputes can be resolved through conferences or hearings, with reinstatement or compensation
(capped at six months' pay) as potential outcomes.
Businesses can face significant challenges due to such claims. Many prefer to avoid the risk by hiring
casual workers or tightening employment contracts, including job descriptions, probation periods, and
measurable targets. Unfair dismissal claims can damage a business's reputation and lead to costly
settlements. As a result, many businesses seek specialist advice to navigate termination procedures.

The resolution of disputes should ideally be achieved with a minimum of time and money diverted
away from productivity.

Disputes can be:
Overt - Public and potentially legal action take
Covert- Conflicts that are only recognised by the workplace itself and dealt with quietly

● Lightning strike- Without an employer being notified
● Lockout - Employer closes the entrance to the workplace

Negotiation
- Method of resolving disputes when discussions between the parties result in a compromise and
a formal or informal agreement.
Most Australian industrial disputes are resolved by resumption of work, without negotiation, such as
following a stop- work meeting.
Mediation
- Mediation is the confidential discussion of issues in a non-threatening environment, in the
presence of a neutral, objective third party.
It allows the parties to become empowered by resolving their own disputes, and it reduces the risk of
disputes escalating and leading to expensive legal costs or industrial action.

Involvement of courts
Industrial disputes that escalate to the level of courts and tribunals are most likely to occur when
disputes have passed their nominal expiry date, bargaining has commenced towards a new agreement,
and negotiations have failed.
Conciliation - process where a third party is involved in helping two other parties reach an
agreement.
Arbitration - process where a third party hears both sides of a dispute and makes a legally binding
decision to resolve the dispute.
Tribunals
Courts are used for resolving general civil and criminal matters, such as contract disputes, personal
injury claims, and serious offences, following strict legal processes.
Tribunals are used for handling specific issues like employment disputes, immigration cases, or
administrative reviews, offering quicker and less formal resolutions.

through dismissal or retrenchment. It is often shown as a percentage of total staff numbers. Within
Australia, the turnover rate averages around 12–15 percent per year, and fluctuates with the economic
cycle; however, in the hospitality industry, turnover is 90%. A high staff turnover could be the result
of push factors such as a bad boss or a poorly designed job or pull factors such as further opportunities
and promotions.
